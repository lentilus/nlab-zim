Definition

In propositional logic, the contrapositive of an implication P→QP\to Q is the converse implication (¬Q)→(¬P)(\neg Q) \to (\neg P) of negations.

The contrapositive rule states that it is valid to derive the contrapositive ¬Q→¬P\neg{Q} \to \neg{P} from the original statement P→QP \to Q (where ¬\neg is negation and →\to is implication). In symbols:

P→Q¬Q→¬PCP. \frac {P \to Q} {\neg{Q} \to \neg{P}} \;CP \mathrlap{\,.}

The combination of this rule, followed by modus ponens (MP, the elimination rule for implication) was traditionally called modus tollens:

P→Q¬Q→¬PCP¬Q¬PMP. \frac{ \displaystyle \frac {P \to Q} {\neg{Q} \to \neg{P}} \;CP \;\;\; \neg{Q} } {\neg{P}} \;\; MP \mathrlap{\,.}

The contrapositive rule is valid in intuitionistic logic, not just in classical logic; however, the reverse rule is valid only in classical logic (apply the reverse rule to the consequent of ¬P→(¬Q→¬P)\neg{P}\to(\neg{Q}\to\neg{P}); the dreaded ¬¬P→P\neg\neg{P}\to{P} then easily follows from ¬¬P→(¬P→¬¬¬P)\neg\neg{P}\to(\neg{P}\to\neg\neg\neg{P}) with another use of the reverse rule).

Another intuitionistically valid rule, this one reversible, is

P→¬QQ→¬P, \frac {P \to \neg{Q}} {Q \to \neg{P} \mathrlap{\,,}}

as both statements are equivalent to the negation of P∧QP \wedge Q (where ∧\wedge is conjunction). However, the superficially similar

¬P→Q¬Q→P \frac {\neg{P} \to Q} {\neg{Q} \to P}

is again valid only classically.
