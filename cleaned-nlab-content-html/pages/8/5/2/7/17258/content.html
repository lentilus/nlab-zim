Idea

Kinship is the collection of relations, rules and behaviors concerning alliance and fictive or real consanguinity that occur in the social organisation of the biological reproduction of individuals. Kinship relations play a significant role for individuals as well as for society as a whole.

In particular in pre-industrial societies, systems of marriage and kinship are often cast into rigid and intricate patterns that are a preferred playground for applications of mathematics to anthropology ever since André Weil contributed an algebraic appendix to Claude Lévi-Strauss' groundbreaking Les structures élémentaires de la parenté.

Permutation models

It is generally assumed that all societies explicitly impose some restrictions on the types of possible marriages. The resulting kinship systems then differ whether these restrictions take on the form of prohibitions like e.g. in modern societies that merely ban certain marriages due to considerations of consanguinity or age, or the form of prescriptions that specify positively to marry into a determinate ‘marriage class’ or category of kins.

The latter prescriptive systems have been called elementary structures by Lévi-Strauss and further divided into systems of restricted exchange where two groups A,BA,B directly exchange spouses, or systems of generalized exchange with longer cycles of exchange e.g. group AA gives spouses to BB that gives spouses to CC that gives spouses to AA.

Since marriages become deterministic on the classes, elementary kinship systems are amenable to analysis via permutations on the set of marriage classes. This group-theoretic modeling of elementary structures has been pioneered by André Weil (1949). Here is Lévi-Strauss on Weil’s contribution:

Historiquement parlant, ces pages ont une grande importance. Toute la mathématique de la parenté qui s’est beaucoup développée depuis en est sortie. Et cela continue.1

Weil’s ideas have later been systematized by Philippe Courrège and others.

Definition

An (elementary) kinship system is a triple (S,ω,μ)(S,\omega,\mu) where SS is a finite set, and ω,μ\omega,\mu are permutations of SS.

A morphism of kinship systems (S 1,ω 1,μ 1)→(S 2,ω 2,μ 2)(S_1,\omega_1,\mu_1)\to (S_2,\omega_2,\mu_2) is a function f:S 1→S 2f:S_1\to S_2 that is equivariant with respect to ω\omega and μ\mu i.e. f∘ω 1=ω 2∘ff\circ\omega_1 =\omega_2\circ f and f∘μ 1=μ 2∘ff\circ\mu_1 =\mu_2\circ f.

The resulting category 𝒦\mathcal{K} is called the category of kinship (systems).

Remark

The axiomatics was proposed in Courrège (1965) with the intended interpretation that SS represents the set of (disjoint) marriage classes, that the conjugal function ω\omega links the men in class xx to the class ω(x)\omega (x) that contains their wifes (’men in xx marry women in ω(x)\omega (x)’) and that the matrilineal function μ\mu links the mothers in class xx to the class μ(x)\mu (x) that contains their children (’women in xx have children in μ(x)\mu(x)’).

One can then define a patrilineal function2 π:\pi:=μ∘ω=\mu\circ\omega that links the fathers in xx to their children in μ(ω(x))\mu(\omega (x)) (’men in xx have children in μ(ω(x))\mu(\omega (x))’).

Since a kinship structure (S,ω,μ)(S,\omega,\mu) contains an element ω\omega of ‘alliance’ and an element μ\mu of ‘descent’ one could view the axiomatics as a reconciliation of the Anglo-Saxon tradition in kinship studies that stressed descent and Lévi-Strauss’ views that stressed alliance. But this ‘reconciliation’ is more one of appearances as one could equally well use π\pi and μ\mu as undefined terms and define ω=μ −1∘π\omega =\mu^{-1}\circ \pi.

Given a system of kinship (S,ω,μ)(S,\omega,\mu), the subgroup GG of Aut(S)Aut(S) generated by ω\omega and μ\mu is called the group of kinship terms of (S,ω,μ)(S,\omega,\mu). The system (S,ω,μ)(S,\omega,\mu) is called irreducible (resp. regular) when the natural action of GG on SS is transitive (resp. free) i.e. :

  • For all x,y∈Sx,y\in S there is a g∈Gg\in G such that gx=yg x=y.

Some terminology and properties

A system S=(S,ω,μ)∈𝒦\mathbf{S}=(S,\omega,\mu)\in \mathcal{K} is called exogamic when ω≠id\omega\neq id, and endogamic otherwise. This simply says that spouses always stem from the same marriage class (resp. from different classes).

S\mathbf{S} is called matrilineal (resp. patrilineal) when μ=id\mu = id (resp. π=id\pi = id). This says that the child is in the same class as the mother (resp. as the father).

S\mathbf{S} is called a system of restricted exchange if ω 2=id\omega^2=id, a system of generalized exchange otherwise.

A particular important category of kin are cousins e.g. in many Western societies they are the closest type of kins eligible for marriage whereas in Arabian societies patrilateral parallel cousin marriage is often the preferred type of marriage.

This can be viewed as a consequence of the property that the cousin category is at the intersection of important poles of kinship relations as affinity and non-affinity, symmetry and asymmetry (in the case of cross-cousins e.g. the father’sister’s daughter from male perspective and the mother’s brother’s son in reverse direction). This warrants

Definition

A system S=(S,ω,μ)∈𝒦\mathbf{S}=(S,\omega,\mu)\in \mathcal{K} is said to admit marriage with the (female) matrilateral cross-cousin if

  • ωμ=μω\omega\mu = \mu\omega (MCC)

S\mathbf{S} is said to admit marriage with the (female) patrilateral cross-cousin if

  • ωμ=μω −1\omega\mu = \mu\omega^{-1} (PCC)

A direct calculation yields

Proposition

Let S=(S,ω,μ)∈𝒦\mathbf{S}=(S,\omega,\mu)\in \mathcal{K} satisfy MCC. S\mathbf{S} satifies PCC iff S\mathbf{S} is of restricted exchange. ▪\qed

Since ω\omega and μ\mu generate the group GG of kinship terms the following is immediate

Proposition

A system S=(S,ω,μ)∈𝒦\mathbf{S}=(S,\omega,\mu)\in \mathcal{K} satisfies MCC iff the group GG of kinship terms of S\mathbf{S} is commutative. ▪\qed

In fact, the fullsubcategory ℳ⊂𝒦\mathcal{M}\subset \mathcal{K} of kinships systems satisfying MCC is equivalent to the topos of ℤ×ℤ\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}-actions, where ℤ×ℤ\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z} is the free Abelian group on two generators because actions of F 2F_2 such that GG is commutative correspond precisely to actions of ℤ×ℤ\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}.

A more general way to view this, is to consider the surjective homomorphism α:F 2→ℤ×ℤ\alpha:F_2\to \mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z} viewed as a functor that sends the generators 1,21,2 to the generators (1,0),(0,1)(1,0),(0,1), respectively. Topos-theoretic perspectives

In our exposition above we have already taken advantage of the categorical point of view advanced in Lawvere and Schanuel (1997, 1999) by adapting it to the classical algebraic approach to kinship. In this section we summarize their ideas in the original setting.

Lawvere and Schanuel proposed to study the concept of kinship starting from the notion of a (filiation structured) society modeled mathematically as a set XX (of individuals) together with two endofunctions m,fm,f that assign to an individual x∈Xx\in X his mother m(x)∈Xm(x)\in X resp. his father f(x)∈Xf(x)\in X.

If one organizes these sets with filiation structure into a category the result is a topos namely the topos Set M 2 opSet^{M_2^{op}} of right actions of the free monoid M 2M_2 on two symbols {m,f}\{m,f\}.

Since the parental endofunctions m,fm,f are completely unconstrained3, the resulting ‘kinship relations’ are equally unconstrained, e.g. nothing prevents an individual’s mother from coinciding with the same individual’s father etc. It therefore becomes advantageous to equip the ‘societies’ with further structure. Lawvere and Schanuel propose to achieve this by introducing appropriate labeling objects LL and passage to the slice topos Set M 2 op/LSet^{M_2^{op}}/L.

A minimal model of a society that at least keeps the gender of the parents apart is the set G={female,male}G=\{female, male\} consisting of a ‘female’ and a distinct ‘male’ individual4 together with parental functions m,fm,f complying with the usual gender rules e.g. m(female)=femalem(female)=female, f(female)=malef(female)=male etc. Then a consistent gender distinction on the parental structure of an arbitrary society corresponds to a structure preserving map to GG i.e. an object of the slice topos Set M 2 op/GSet^{M_2^{op}}/G.

Similarly, exogamy can be modeled by a clans object CC e.g. consisting of two clans C={bear,wolf}C=\{bear, wolf\} obeying matrilinearity in the sense that the mother-of function mm is constant but the father-of function ff switches the clans expressing thereby that the father of an individual comes from another clan than the individual and his mother.5

By labeling with the product G×CG\times C one can achieve gender distinction and exogamy at the same time:

G×C=he−wolf ⇆ff he−bear m↓ f↗↖f ↓m she−wolfm↺ she−bearm↺ G\times C \quad=\qquad\array{ he-wolf &\overset{f}{\underset{f}{\leftrightarrows}}& he-bear\\ _{m}\downarrow&_{f}{\nearrow} {\nwarrow}{_{f}}& \downarrow{_{m}}\\ \underset{\overset{\circlearrowleft}{m}}{she-wolf}& &\underset{\overset{\circlearrowleft}{m}}{she-bear} }\quad

Occasionally, one can exploit the “biological unreasonability” inherent in the unconstrained parental mappings m,fm,f: e.g. the Na society in Yunnan (China) is a fatherless society lacking marriage or social acknowledgement of male contribution to procreation (cf. Godelier, pp.494-509).

The society consists of matrilineal families consisting of a woman, her brothers and her children. Conception takes place via “nocturnal male visitors” from other houses. In first approximation we can model this absence of fatherhood by the triviality of the father-of-map f=idf=id: every individual xx is his own father. This corresponds to the full subcategory of societies of form (X,m:X→X,id X)(X,m:X\to X, id_X) which is equivalent to Set M 1 opSet^{M_1^{op}} where M 1M_1 is the free monoid generated by {m}\{m\}.

This subcategory is the image under the full and faithful q *q^* arising from the adjunction

q !⊣q *⊣q *:Set M 2 op→Set M 1 op q_!\dashv q^*\dashv q_*: Set^{M_2^{op}}\to Set^{M_1^{op}}

induced by the surjective morphism of monoids q:M 2→M 1q:M_2\to M_1 with q(f)=1q(f)=1 and q(m)=mq(m)=m.

Even societies that entertain an elaborate pattern of kinship relations and terminologies tend to blur distinctions with increasing genealogical depth e.g. whereas parallel and cross kin differ terminologically in the parental generation G +1G^{+1}, they are named with the same term in the generation G +2G^{+2} of the grandparents. This lumping together of generations G ≤iG^{\leq i} to a cluster of ‘ancestors’ is achieved by quotienting M 2M_2 with relations expressing the equivalence G +j≅G +(j+1)G^{+j}\cong G^{+(j+1)} for i≤ji\leq j e.g. for i=2i=2 this amounts to equate all expressions of depth 33 with an appropriate expression of depth 22: f 3=fmf=f 2f^3=fmf=f^2 etc. The resulting epimorphism of monoids α\alpha:M 2→M ≈M_2\to M_\approx induces functors α !⊣α *⊣α *:Set M 2op→Set M ≈ op\alpha_!\dashv \alpha^*\dashv\alpha_*:Set^{M_2{^{op}}}\to Set^{{M_\approx}^{op}} like above.

At the end of his paper, Lawvere makes the interesting suggestion to use as abstract general not the category corresponding to M 2M_2 but the category 𝒞\mathcal{C} based on the diagram of three parallel arrows

•→→→• \bullet{\underset{\rightarrow}{\overset{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}}}\bullet

The result still contains the topos of M 2M_2-actions as a quotient topos because inverting one of the arrows turns the resulting diagram up to equivalence into the diagram of one point with two endomaps. Hence the objects in topos Set 𝒞 opSet^{\mathcal{C}^{op}} permit a more finely grained analysis than the objects in the topos of M 2M_2-actions.
