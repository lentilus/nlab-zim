Idea

What is called the “geometrization of quantum mechanics” by Kibble (1979) who introduced the idea, or “geometrical formulation of quantum mechanics” [Ashtekar & Schilling (1999)] or just “geometric quantum mechanics” [Brody & Hughston (2001)], but which ought to be called the “symplectic formulation”, is the observation that, in standard quantum mechanics:

  1. the complex projective space PℋP \mathscr{H} of the Hilbert space of pure quantum states ℋ\mathscr{H} is canonically a Kähler manifold (via the Fubini-Study metric) and so in particular a symplectic manifold,

  2. the curves in PℋP \mathscr{H} which correspond to solutions of the Schrödinger equation are Hamiltonian flows with respect to this symplectic structure.

This is fairly immediate to see from mathematical inspection, but the perspective is somewhat surprising from the point of view of standard accounts of classical/quantum physics, which tend to frame symplectic geometry and its Hamiltonian flows as the hallmark of classical phase spaces and classical physics, and to highlight quantization as a deformation of this symplectic structure (whence: “deformation quantization”).

It follows that instead of thinking of a quantization of a symplectic manifold (M,ω)(M,\omega) as some kind of deformation to operators on a Hilbert space, one can think of it as merely replacing the classical mechanics of MM with the classical mechanics of P(ℋ).P(\mathcal{H}).

Now, the standard perspective is certainly not wrong, but various authors have inevitably suggested that the “geometrical formulation” (more descriptive would be: “symplectic formulation”) of quantum mechanics may point to some deeper truth, and if only to show some kind of conceptual unity where one is used to amplifying the dichotomy.

Explanation

Let (M 2n,ω)(M^{2n},\omega) be a symplectic manifold and let dμd\mu be a measure on MM (which is always of the form hω nh\omega^n for some smooth function hh). Let ℒ→M\mathcal{L}\to M be a prequantum line bundle and let Δ:M 3→ℂ\Delta:M^3\to\mathbb{C} be a smooth function.

Then there is an equivalence of categories between:

  1. (Path integral quantization) Quantizations obtained via a path integral over curves in M,M, whose amplitude is given by parallel transport and whose 3-point function is Δ.\Delta.

  2. (Geometric quantization) The category of Hilbert spaces given by a subspace of sections of the prequantum line bundle, such that pointwise evaluation is continuous and whose curvature is VE(logΔ)VE(\log{\Delta}) (VEVE is the van Est map).

  3. (Berezin quantization) Maps

    (1)q:M→P(ℋ)⊂B(ℋ), q \colon M \to P(\mathcal{H})\subset B(\mathcal{H}),

    with the overcompleteness property:

    (2)1 ℋ=∫ Mqdμ 1_{\mathcal{H}} = \textstyle{\int_M} q\,d\mu

    and whose 3-point function is Δ.\Delta. Here we are using the identification of points in projective space with rank–one orthogonal projections.

Item 2 presents by far the most common way of thinking about quantization, with the other two (especially 1) being nearly completely overlooked. However, it can be argued that 2 is really just a way of computing 3 or 1, where the latter is the only one which really generalizes to Poisson manifolds. This is because the path integral is a section of

π 0 *ℒ *⊗π 1 *ℒ→M×M,\pi_0^*\mathcal{L}^*\otimes \pi_1^*\mathcal{L}\to M\times M\,,

and for Poisson manifolds we can replace M×MM\times M by the symplectic groupoid. More specifically, the path integral is a certain Hermitian, idempotent section of the twisted convolution algebra. For symplectic manifolds, it is formally given by

Ω(a,b)=∫ γ(0)=a γ(1)=b𝒟γP(γ)∈Hom(ℒ a,ℒ b),\Omega(a,b)=\int_{\gamma(0)=a}^{\gamma(1)=b}\mathcal{D}\gamma\,P(\gamma)\in\text{Hom}(\mathcal{L}_{a},\mathcal{L}_{b})\,,

where P(γ)P(\gamma) is parallel transport over γ.\gamma.

In the most common examples, item 2 is given by holomorphic sections of a Kähler manifold. In item 3, qq is a classifying map for ℒ\mathcal{L} and points in its image are called coherent states – it is not just any classifying map though, it has an overcompleteness property. This is Berezin‘s formulation of quantization, whereas item 1 is how Klauder conceived of quantization. For a quote:

“all these different procedures to quantize phase space [can physically] be thought of as just different ways of regularizing and defining properly the path integral” [Davide Gaiotto, yt]

That something like this was true seems to have been first made explicit in Odzijewicz 1992, who stated a related equivalence of categories and the relationship to path integrals.

On Complex Projective Space

The simplest and most natural example of this is when ℋ\mathcal{H} is finite dimensional and M = P ( ℋ ) M=P(\mathcal{H}) with ω\omega being the Fubini–Study form. Item 1 and item 3 are very natural to describe here, while item 2 is a bit more complicated but quickly follows from the description of 1. We’ll first describe item 3.

In this example, qq (1) is the identity. It’s not immediately obvious that this example satisfies the overcompleteness property (2), but it does with respect to the Fubini–Study measure (up to a constant). There is a natural map

⟨⟩:B(ℋ)→C ∞(P(ℋ)),⟨H⟩([v])=⟨v|H|v⟩, \langle\rangle \,\colon\, B(\mathcal{H}) \to C^{\infty}\big(P(\mathcal{H})\big) \,,\quad \langle H\rangle\big([v]\big) \,=\, \langle v|H|v\rangle \,,

where vv is any normalized representative of [v][v]. This is just the expectation value of HH in the state [v][v]. The map (multiplied by the imaginary unit i\mathrm{i}) is a homomorphism of Lie algebras, with respect to the commutator on B(ℋ)B(\mathcal{H}) and the Poisson bracket on P(ℋ)P(\mathcal{H}). This is a bit surprising, because it is so commonly stated that there is no physically reasonable morphism of Lie algebras in the other direction, while this direction receives relatively little attention. The significance of i⟨⟩\mathrm{i}\langle \rangle being a Lie algebra morphism is that, with respect to classical observables of the form ⟨H⟩,\langle H\rangle\,, the classical and quantum mechanics are the same. That is,

i∂∂tΨ=HΨ \mathrm{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi \,=\, H\Psi

if and only if t↦[Ψ](t)t\mapsto [\Psi](t) is the Hamiltonian flow of the Hamiltonian vector field of ⟨H⟩\langle H\rangle. Equivalently, A∈B(ℋ)A\in B(\mathcal{H}) satisfies Heisenberg's equation of motion with respect to HH if and only if ⟨A⟩\langle A\rangle satisfies Hamilton's equations of motion with respect to ⟨H⟩.\langle H\rangle.

There is also a natural map from classically mixed states to quantum mixed states, ie. density matrices. It is given by

f↦Q f=∫ P(ℋ)fqω n, f\mapsto Q_f \,=\, \int_{P(\mathcal{H})} f\,q\,\omega^n \,,

where f≥0f\ge 0 and

∫ P(ℋ)fω n=1. \int_{P(\mathcal{H})}f\,\omega^n \,=\, 1\,.

From the overcompleteness property (#OvercompletenessProperty), it follows that

Tr(Q fA)=∫ P(ℋ)f⟨A⟩ω n. Tr(Q_{f}A) \,=\, \int_{P(\mathcal{H})} f\,\langle A\rangle \omega^n\,.

This means that ⟨⟩\langle \rangle also preserves the state structure. Mathematically, ⟨⟩=Q †\langle\rangle=Q^{\dagger}, where the inner product is the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product. Q fQ_f is known as Berezin‘s contravariant symbol, and ⟨⟩\langle \rangle is known as Berezin’s covariant symbol. Of course, QQ is really defined on any reasonably nice function — in general it should be interpreted as an operator, not a state. QQ agrees with the Berezin–Toeplitz quantization [Schlichenmaier 2010]. Let us emphasize that f≥0⇒Q f≥0f\ge 0\implies Q_f\ge 0 and

Tr(Q f)=∫ P(ℋ)fω n. Tr(Q_{f}) \,=\, \int_{P(\mathcal{H})} f\, \omega^n\,.

These are important properties of a quantization map to have, since this is exactly what is needed for it to preserve states. If QQ is an abstract quantization map with this property, then QQ is compatible with a prequantum line bundle if and only if QQ preserves pure states. By the overcompleteness property, it’s similarly true that ⟨⟩\langle \rangle intertwines the trace with integration, so it also preserves states — however, it certainly doesn’t preserve pure states! ⟨⟩\langle\rangle maps quantum pure states to classical mixed states, this introduces uncertainty.

The 3–point function (which is really a degree 2 cochain on the pair groupoid and determines a class in groupoid cohomology) determines the first Chern class of the prequantum line bundle and is given by

Δ([x],[y],[z])=⟨x|y⟩⟨y|z⟩⟨z|x⟩. \Delta([x],[y],[z]) \,=\, \langle x|y\rangle\langle y|z\rangle\langle z|x\rangle\,.

This is the same 3–point function that appears in the Poisson sigma-model in the context of formal deformation quantization.

As for item 1, the line bundle is the canonical line bundle and the path integral

Ω([x],[y])=∫ γ(0)=[x] γ(1)=[y]𝒟γP(γ)∈Hom(ℒ x,ℒ y)\Omega([x],[y])=\int_{\gamma(0)=[x]}^{\gamma(1)=[y]}\mathcal{D}\gamma\,P(\gamma)\in\text{Hom}(\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{L}_{y})

is the map ⟨x|↦⟨x|y⟩⟨y|.\langle x|\mapsto \langle x|y\rangle\langle y|\,.

There are now two ways of describing item 2. The traditional way is by taking the Hilbert space to be that of holomorphic sections. The second way of describing it is by taking the Hilbert space to be the image of the orthogonal projection defined by the path integal, ie.

Ψ↦([y]↦∫ P(ℋ)Ψ([x])|y⟩⟨y|ω n).\Psi\mapsto\Big([y]\mapsto\int_{P(\mathcal{H})}\Psi([x])|y\rangle\langle y|\omega^n\Big)\,.

These two Hilbert spaces are the same as this is the orthogonal projection onto holomorphic sections.

This tells most of the story for P(ℋ).P(\mathcal{H}).

Projective Submanifolds

There is another class of examples that is just as natural and almost just as simple as P(ℋ).P(\mathcal{H}). These are symplectic submanifolds q:M 2n↪P(ℋ)⊂B(ℋ)q:M^{2n}\xhookrightarrow{} P(\mathcal{H})\subset B(\mathcal{H}) which satisfy the overcompleteness condition, ie. up to a constant

1 ℋ=∫ Mqω n.1_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_M q\omega^n\,.

These are more interesting because the quantum mechanics and classical mechanics are different, ie. i⟨⟩i\langle \rangle is not a morphism of Lie algebras. However, there is a Lie subalgebra on which it is an injective morphism, and otherwise all other formulas discussed in the previous section remain valid. This Lie subalgebra can be identified with the set of operators which, under Schrodinger’s equation, preserve M.M.

One can think of P(ℋ)P(\mathcal{H}) as the pure states, and q(M)q(M) as the subset of pure states that are classical states.

The General Case

In general then, to quantize as a symplectic manifold MM it is enough to embed it into P(ℋ)P(\mathcal{H}) in such a way that the embedded submanifold has the overcompleteness property. It’s not clear to what extent this can be done exactly, however it can be done for Kahler manifolds which have a nice enough group of symmetries, eg. T *ℝ nT^*\mathbb{R}^n and the upper half plane. However, for all prequantizable compact manifolds (and many non–compact ones) this can be done approximately, ie.

Corollary

Let (M,ω)(M,\omega) be a prequantizable compact symplectic manifold. Then there exists a sequence of embeddings

q k:M↪P(ℋ k)q_k:M\xhookrightarrow{}P(\mathcal{H}_k)

such that

‖q k *ω FS/k−ω‖ C m=𝒪(1/k)\|q_k^*\omega_{FS}/k-\omega\|_{C^m}=\mathcal{O}(1/k)

for all m∈ℕ,m\in\mathbb{N}, and such that q k(M)q_k(M) is overcomplete with respect to a measure dμ kd\mu_k for which dμ k/k n→ω n.d\mu_{k}/k^n \to \omega^n\,.

Here, ω FS\omega_{FS} is the Fubini–Study form. This theorem identifies the structure used to quantize and get star products on general compact symplectic manifolds, Borthwicke & Uribe, Schlichenmaier. This theorem follows from theorem 1.1 of Dai–Liu–Ma, theorem 3.6 of Ma–Marinescu, and the observation that the symplectic Kodaira embedding is overcomplete.

Note that, there don’t seem to be any (convincing?) examples of quantizations of symplectic manifolds which can’t be strenghtened to a quantization of this form, so this perspective seems universal. Eg. Consider the quantization of T *ℝT^*\mathbb{R} using the polarization with x=const.x=const. One can define coherent states using the lowering operator x^+ip^\hat{x}+i\hat{p} and then one immediately gets all of this additional structure.

Relationship to Kostant-Souriau Operators

Under the equivalence of categories from item 3 to item 2,

⟨x|→([y]↦⟨x|y⟩⟨y|)\langle x|\to \big([y]\mapsto \langle x|y\rangle\langle y|\big)

gives a map I:ℋ→Γ(M,ℒ).I:\mathcal{H}\to \Gamma(M,\mathcal{L}). If H∈B(ℋ)H\in B(\mathcal{H}) is such that the Hamiltonian vector field X ⟨H⟩X_{\langle H\rangle} is tangent to q:M↪P(ℋ),q:M\xhookrightarrow{} P(\mathcal{H}), then

H=I −1∘(∇ X ⟨H⟩+i⟨H⟩)∘I.H=I^{-1}\circ (\nabla_{X_{\langle H\rangle}}+i\langle H\rangle)\circ I\,.

Therefore, i⟨⟩i\langle\rangle extends the Kostant-Souriau prequantization map. Note that, generically the Kostant-Souriau prequantization map doesn’t work at all, ie. it doesn’t quantize any non–constant functions! Eg. a generic genus g≥3g\ge 3 Riemann surface has no nontrivial automorphisms, and therefore on a generic genus g≥3g\ge 3 surface the Kostant-Souriau prequantization map only quantizes constants. As a result, it only gives operators which are constant multiples of the identity.
